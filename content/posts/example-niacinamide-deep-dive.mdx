Niacinamide has become one of the most-discussed actives in skincare, and for once, the hype is largely justified. But "largely" is doing real work in that sentence. This piece goes through what the clinical literature actually shows, where the gaps are, and how to think about using it.

## What is niacinamide

Niacinamide is the amide form of vitamin B3 (nicotinic acid). Unlike nicotinic acid, it does not cause the vasodilatory "flush" response, making it well-tolerated even at higher concentrations. In the body, it's a precursor to NAD+ and NADP+, coenzymes central to cellular energy metabolism.

Topically applied niacinamide penetrates the stratum corneum and epidermis. It doesn't need to be converted to another form to exert its effects at the skin level — its mechanisms operate at the cellular and intercellular level directly.

## Mechanisms of action

### Barrier strengthening

One of niacinamide's most clinically consistent effects is the upregulation of key structural proteins in the epidermis. Studies show it increases synthesis of:

- **Ceramides** — the dominant lipid class in the stratum corneum, accounting for roughly 50% of its lipid content
- **Involucrin and filaggrin** — proteins critical to corneocyte maturation and NMF (natural moisturising factor) production
- **Fatty acids and cholesterol** — the other two major lipid classes that complete the lamellar structure

The net effect is a thicker, more organised stratum corneum with better transepidermal water loss (TEWL) control. This has been demonstrated in both ex vivo models and in-vivo trials, including a study by Bissett et al. showing measurable TEWL improvement over a 12-week treatment period.

<Callout type="science">
A 2004 study (Tanno et al.) found that 2% niacinamide applied twice daily for 4 weeks significantly increased ceramide and free fatty acid levels in the stratum corneum compared to vehicle control. The effect was concentration-dependent up to approximately 5%.
</Callout>

### Hyperpigmentation reduction

This is the most commercially marketed benefit, and the evidence is solid — with an important mechanistic nuance.

Niacinamide does not inhibit tyrosinase (the enzyme that produces melanin) in the way that kojic acid or arbutin do. Instead, it works downstream: it inhibits the transfer of melanosomes from melanocytes to keratinocytes. This means melanin is still produced, but less of it reaches the upper skin layers where it manifests as visible pigmentation.

The clinical implication is that results take longer to appear (the pigment already transferred has to cycle out naturally) but the mechanism is well-tolerated and doesn't carry the irritation risk of tyrosinase-inhibiting actives.

Multiple randomised controlled trials have confirmed efficacy at 4–5% concentrations for:
- Post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation (PIH)
- Melasma (as adjunct or standalone)
- Solar lentigines (age spots)

<Callout type="note">
One frequently cited Japanese study (Hakozaki et al., 2002) found that 5% niacinamide reduced skin lightness by approximately 35–68% compared to vehicle after 8 weeks in a double-blind, randomised design.
</Callout>

### Sebum regulation

Sebum overproduction is a major driver of acne and enlarged-pore appearance. Several studies have found that 2–4% niacinamide reduces sebum excretion rate (SER) when measured via sebumeter — a validated clinical instrument.

The mechanism isn't fully characterised, but the leading hypothesis involves niacinamide's role in lipid metabolism within sebocytes. It may also have an anti-inflammatory effect that reduces the sebocyte's response to pro-inflammatory stimuli.

### Anti-inflammatory effects

Niacinamide inhibits the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines and reduces surface levels of certain immunoglobulins implicated in rosacea-like reactions. It also suppresses protein glycation — relevant to skin yellowing and stiffening associated with chronological aging.

This makes it useful as a background anti-inflammatory in most skin types, and particularly valuable for those with rosacea or reactive skin who cannot tolerate stronger actives.

## Effective concentrations

<Callout type="science">
The bulk of evidence for niacinamide efficacy clusters at 4–5%. Lower concentrations (2%) show measurable effects on TEWL and barrier function but weaker efficacy on pigmentation. Concentrations above 5% do not appear to significantly increase benefit and may increase the (low) risk of flushing in sensitive individuals.
</Callout>

Most well-formulated commercial products sit in the 5–10% range. At 10%, evidence for incremental benefit over 5% is limited; some individuals report temporary redness at very high concentrations, though this is uncommon.

The upshot: 4–5% is the evidence-supported sweet spot for most use cases. Products at 10% are not harmful, but you're likely not getting materially more benefit.

## Formulation considerations

### pH compatibility

Niacinamide is stable across a wide pH range (3–9) and is not itself pH-sensitive. However, a historical concern exists about layering it with vitamin C: under conditions of very low pH, niacinamide and ascorbic acid can theoretically form a complex (nicotinic acid + dehydroascorbic acid) that causes facial flushing.

<Callout type="warning">
The niacinamide + vitamin C incompatibility concern is largely theoretical at real-world product concentrations and pH. Studies examining this interaction directly have found no clinically relevant issue when using products at typical formulation pH (3.5–5.5). That said, layering a pH 2.5 vitamin C serum directly followed by a niacinamide product could cause transient redness in sensitive skin — waiting 10–15 minutes eliminates this concern entirely.
</Callout>

### The vitamin C myth

The full story on niacinamide and vitamin C:

The 1969 paper often cited to support incompatibility was conducted at elevated temperatures (100°C) with purified compounds — conditions bearing no resemblance to topical application. More recent work has found that at concentrations below 10% and typical storage temperatures, complex formation is minimal and clinically irrelevant.

You do not need to avoid using niacinamide and vitamin C in the same routine. They can even be combined in the same formula without issue.

## What niacinamide cannot do

Being clear about limitations matters:

- It is **not** an exfoliant. It doesn't change skin cell turnover rate.
- It does **not** stimulate collagen synthesis in the way that retinoids do. Some studies show modest anti-aging effects, but these are secondary to other mechanisms.
- It will **not** treat moderate-to-severe acne as a standalone. It can support acne management as an anti-inflammatory but is not equivalent to benzoyl peroxide or prescription retinoids.
- **Deep or dermal pigmentation** (melasma with dermal component, some post-inflammatory changes) responds poorly to niacinamide — addressing these typically requires combination approaches with tretinoin or azelaic acid.

## Protocol recommendations

For most people, applying a 5% niacinamide serum or moisturiser once or twice daily is sufficient. It layers well with virtually every other skincare ingredient (the vitamin C concern notwithstanding), requires no special timing, and can be used AM, PM, or both.

Realistic timelines:
- **Barrier improvement** — 4–6 weeks of consistent use
- **Sebum/pore appearance** — 6–8 weeks
- **Hyperpigmentation** — 8–12 weeks for visible change; full response may take 16+ weeks
